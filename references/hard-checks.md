# Hard Checks: Novelty, Coverage, Synthesis

Use these checks early. A review that fails one hard check may still contain useful material, but its central publication claim may be unstable.

# 1. Novelty

## Question

What does this review add beyond the closest existing reviews?

## Evidence to inspect

- manuscript statements about prior reviews;
- comparison with the closest review papers;
- differences in scope, time window, corpus, method, synthesis, framework, or engineering decision value;
- whether the claimed new contribution is actually used in the Results/Discussion rather than only announced in the Introduction.

## Strong novelty examples

- a new comparison rule that changes which studies can legitimately be compared;
- a reproducible taxonomy that reveals a previously hidden pattern;
- quantitative evidence mapping that produces a new field-level result;
- a mechanism-based synthesis that resolves apparently contradictory findings;
- a new methodology framework that changes experimental design or interpretation;
- a defensible update that materially changes conclusions because the evidence base has changed.

## Weak novelty signals

- "no previous review is comprehensive" without comparison;
- a narrower title but essentially the same analysis;
- reorganising familiar topics into new headings;
- adding more recent papers without showing that conclusions change;
- calling a descriptive diagram a framework without operational rules.

## Result

**Pass** — distinct contribution is evidence-backed and used.

**Conditional** — likely contribution exists, but comparison with prior reviews or operational proof is incomplete.

**Fail** — no defensible distinction from the closest reviews, or the supposed contribution is mostly repackaging.

# 2. Coverage

## Question

Is the literature corpus defensible enough to support the manuscript's claims?

## Evidence to inspect

- databases and sources;
- search dates and queries;
- inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- screening path;
- citation chasing;
- references cited elsewhere in the manuscript but absent from the core corpus;
- obvious candidate studies found externally;
- treatment of multiple papers from one dataset;
- denominator-based claims.

## High-risk coverage failures

- strong candidate studies satisfy the stated criteria but receive no inclusion/exclusion explanation;
- the manuscript states a precise final `N` but does not show how candidates became that `N`;
- denominator-based percentages rely on an unstable core sample;
- literature-search terms include a concept that may accidentally exclude studies needed to answer the review question;
- background references and core studies are inconsistently separated;
- one paper is counted as one study in one section and multiple experimental datasets elsewhere.

## Important distinction

An unexplained candidate study is not automatically proof that the final sample is wrong.

The appropriate criticism is usually:

> The sample boundary cannot yet be independently verified because this strong candidate is not accounted for.

## Result

**Pass** — the corpus boundary is clear and sufficiently reproducible for the claims made.

**Conditional** — likely adequate, but specific candidates, screening records, or denominator definitions require clarification.

**Fail** — corpus construction is too unstable or opaque to support central quantitative or comparative conclusions.

# 3. Synthesis

## Question

Has the literature been transformed into new knowledge?

## Synthesis ladder

### Level 0 — Inventory

Lists papers, methods, parameters, or findings.

### Level 1 — Comparison

Shows how studies differ or agree.

### Level 2 — Conditional synthesis

Explains that a trend holds only under particular methods, scales, boundary conditions, populations, measurements, or definitions.

### Level 3 — Mechanistic / methodological synthesis

Explains why studies agree or disagree and identifies the governing mechanisms, measurement constraints, or methodological causes.

### Level 4 — Actionable framework

Produces reusable rules, taxonomy, decision logic, evidence mapping, or design guidance that can be applied to new studies.

A strong engineering review usually needs meaningful work above Level 1.

## Evidence to inspect

- cross-study tables;
- figures generated by the review authors;
- contradictions and their explanations;
- normalisation or comparability decisions;
- proposed mechanisms;
- framework or taxonomy application;
- whether research gaps follow from the synthesis.

## Signs of weak synthesis

- one paragraph per paper;
- repeated statements that studies "show different results" without explaining why;
- tables that merely replicate information already visible in the original studies;
- generic recommendations such as "more research is needed";
- a conclusion that repeats section summaries without field-level inference.

## Signs of strong synthesis

- incompatible results are linked to specific boundary conditions;
- the review distinguishes physical disagreement from measurement-definition disagreement;
- non-comparability is itself systematically mapped;
- tables reveal patterns that no individual source contains;
- a framework is demonstrated on real studies and changes what conclusions can be drawn.

## Result

**Pass** — the manuscript generates clear cross-study knowledge.

**Conditional** — synthesis exists but central tables/frameworks require stronger evidence or operationalisation.

**Fail** — the manuscript remains predominantly descriptive or study-by-study.

---

# Hard-check priority rule

When one of these checks fails, prioritise it above stylistic editing.

Typical repair order:

**Coverage → evidence/coding reconstruction → Synthesis → Novelty statement → language**

But if the manuscript is fundamentally indistinguishable from a prior review, Novelty may dominate because rebuilding the corpus alone cannot solve the contribution problem.
